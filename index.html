About This Site

What You'll Find Here

This site presents original research on iCLIP — Injection Continuous Liquid Interface Production — a next-generation 3D printing technology developed at Stanford University and UNC Chapel Hill. By injecting resin directly into the "dead-zone," iCLIP overcomes the mass transportation limits that have long held back conventional continuous printing.

The research explored here is drawn from Lipkowitz et al. (2022), published in Science Advances. You will find a full academic synthesis connecting the key findings of the study, as well as a popular science article written to make this technology accessible to any reader — not just engineers.

Background

The Problem with CLIP

UV vat photopolymerization enables additive manufacturing through 3D printing at 25–100x traditional methods. CLIP, the technology iCLIP is fundamentally built upon, uses oxygen-permeable windows to create a liquid "dead-zone," allowing for cured resin printing at faster-than-traditional speeds.

However, CLIP's primary limitation comes from the amount of mass delivered, bottlenecked by Stefan adhesion forces. Higher grade composite resins with more viscosity are impossible to integrate into CLIP without additional injectors — hence iCLIP.

Research Question

Can viaducts overcome the limit?

Can viaducts be used to actively "inject" into a printing chamber to overcome mass transportation limits and build off of CLIP's foundation?

Hypothesis

Injecting past Stefan forces

Through viaducts at high pressure, it is possible to create an environment which overcomes Stefan adhesion forces, allowing for much greater fabrication speeds, ultra-high viscosity resin printing, and complex multi-material output.

The Answer

Yes — by 5–10x

By injecting resin directly into the "dead-zone," iCLIP increased print speed from CLIP technologies by 5–10x, printed resins up to 3,606 cP, and achieved multi-material geometries in all three dimensions.
